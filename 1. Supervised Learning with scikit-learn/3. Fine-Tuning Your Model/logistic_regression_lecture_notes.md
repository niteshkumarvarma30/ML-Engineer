## 1. Probability Foundations: Bernoulli & Binomial Distributions

Before predicting binary outcomes, we need a mathematical way to model events with two possible results.

### Bernoulli Trial

* **Definition:** A single experiment or event with **only two possible outcomes**:
  * **Success** ($1$) with probability $p$
  * **Failure** ($0$) with probability $1 - p$
* **Examples:** Tossing a coin once (`Heads = 1`, `Tails = 0`), or one student taking an exam (`Pass = 1`, `Fail = 0`).

### Binomial Distribution

* **Definition:** Describes the probability of getting a specific number of successes ($k$) in a fixed number of independent repeated Bernoulli trials ($n$).
* **Formula:**
  $$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$$
* **Where:**
  * $n$ = Total number of trials
  * $k$ = Number of desired successes
  * $p$ = Probability of success on a single trial
  * $1 - p$ = Probability of failure on a single trial
  * $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n - k)!}$ = Number of ways to arrange $k$ successes among $n$ trials

### Worked Example: Coin Toss

Suppose we toss a fair coin **$3$ times** ($n = 3$) and want the probability of getting **exactly $2$ heads** ($k = 2$), where $p = 0.5$:

1. **Set up the formula:**
   $$P(X = 2) = \binom{3}{2} (0.5)^2 (0.5)^{3 - 2}$$
2. **Calculate each component:**
   * $\binom{3}{2} = \frac{3!}{2!1!} = 3$
   * $(0.5)^2 = 0.25$
   * $(0.5)^1 = 0.5$
3. **Multiply together:**
   $$P(X = 2) = 3 \times 0.25 \times 0.5 = 0.375 \quad (37.5\%)$$

## 2. Logistic Regression & The Sigmoid Function

### Core Concept

* **Logistic Regression** (or *logit regression*) is used to predict a **categorical dependent variable** ($Y$).
* For binary classification, the actual outcome is strictly $0$ or $1$:
  $$Y \in \{0, 1\}$$
* Independent variables ($X$) are typically continuous (e.g., hours studied).
* Instead of outputting $0$ or $1$ directly, logistic regression outputs a **predicted probability** ($\hat{y}$ or $\hat{p}$) bounded between $0$ and $1$:
  $$\hat{y} = P(Y = 1 \mid X), \quad \text{where } 0 \le \hat{y} \le 1$$

### The Sigmoid (Logistic) Function

To convert a continuous linear equation into a valid probability between $0$ and $1$, logistic regression uses a two-step process:

1. **Compute a linear score ($z$):**
   $$z = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots + \beta_n x_n$$
2. **Apply the Sigmoid Function $\sigma(z)$:**
   $$p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$
   *(where $e \approx 2.718$ is Euler's number)*
3. **Combined Model Equation:**
   $$P(Y = 1 \mid X) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_n x_n)}}$$

### Key Properties of the S-Shaped Curve

* **At $z = 0$:** $\sigma(0) = 0.5$ (exact $50\%$ midpoint).
* **As $z \to +\infty$:** $\sigma(z) \to 1$ (probability approaches $100\%$).
* **As $z \to -\infty$:** $\sigma(z) \to 0$ (probability approaches $0\%$).

## 3. Training the Model: Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Unlike linear regression—which minimizes squared errors (OLS)—logistic regression estimates its coefficients ($\beta$) using **Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)**. MLE finds the parameter values ($\beta$) that maximize the probability of observing the actual data.

### How the Compact MLE Formula Works (The "Automatic Switch")

Each observation $Y_i$ follows a Bernoulli distribution. The compact formula below is a mathematical shortcut for choosing between $p_i$ (when $Y_i = 1$) and $1 - p_i$ (when $Y_i = 0$) without needing an `if/else` statement:

$$P(Y_i \mid X_i) = p_i^{Y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - Y_i}$$

The exponents act like an automatic switch for any student $i$:

* **When the student passes ($Y_i = 1$):**
  Plug $1$ into the exponents: $p_i^{1} (1 - p_i)^{1 - 1} = p_i^{1} (1 - p_i)^{0}$. Since anything raised to the power of $0$ equals $1$, the second term disappears, leaving just **$p_i$**.
* **When the student fails ($Y_i = 0$):**
  Plug $0$ into the exponents: $p_i^{0} (1 - p_i)^{1 - 0} = p_i^{0} (1 - p_i)^{1}$. Now $p_i^0 = 1$, so the first term disappears, leaving just **$1 - p_i$**.

Assuming all $n$ training observations are independent, the **Likelihood Function** $L(\beta)$ uses the product operator ($\prod$) to multiply these probabilities together across all $n$ observations:

$$L(\beta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{Y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - Y_i}$$

### Worked Example: How Sigmoid Feeds Directly into MLE

**Key Connection:** MLE uses the probabilities generated by the sigmoid function—they aren't two separate things. The sigmoid function creates the model's predicted probabilities ($p_i$), and MLE multiplies together the probabilities of what *actually happened* to each student.

#### Step 1: Start with a guess for the weights ($\beta$)
Test initial weights $\beta_0 = -3$ and $\beta_1 = 1$, giving the linear equation $z = -3 + x$ (where $x$ is hours studied).

#### Step 2: Get sigmoid probabilities ($p_i$) for passing
For each student, plug their hours ($x$) into $z = -3 + x$, and pass $z$ through the sigmoid function $p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ to get their predicted probability of **passing ($y = 1$)**:
* **Student 1 ($x = 1$ hr):** $z = -3 + 1 = -2 \implies p_1 = \frac{1}{1 + e^2} \approx 0.119$

#### Step 3: Match each sigmoid probability to the actual result ($y$)
MLE asks: *"Given our sigmoid probabilities, how likely were we to observe the actual results in our dataset?"*
* **When a student actually passed ($y = 1$):** Use $p_i$ directly (the sigmoid probability of passing).
* **When a student actually failed ($y = 0$):** Use the probability of failing, which is $1 - p_i$.
* For **Student 1**, they actually **failed ($y = 0$)**. Even though the sigmoid gave $p_1 = 0.119$ for passing, the probability of what *actually happened* (failing) is $1 - 0.119 = 0.881$.

#### Summary Table ($z = -3 + x$)

| Student | Hours ($x$) | Actual Result ($y$) | Linear Score ($z = -3 + x$) | Sigmoid Prob ($p_i$) | Prob of Actual Outcome |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1** | $1$ | $0$ (Fail) | $-2$ | $0.119$ | $1 - 0.119 = \mathbf{0.881}$ |
| **2** | $2$ | $0$ (Fail) | $-1$ | $0.269$ | $1 - 0.269 = \mathbf{0.731}$ |
| **3** | $3$ | $1$ (Pass) | $0$ | $0.500$ | $\mathbf{0.500}$ |
| **4** | $4$ | $1$ (Pass) | $1$ | $0.731$ | $\mathbf{0.731}$ |

#### Step 4: Multiply them together for the Likelihood $L(\beta)$
$$L(\beta) = 0.881 \times 0.731 \times 0.500 \times 0.731 \approx 0.235$$

*(MLE continues searching for different sets of weights $\beta_0, \beta_1$ that produce an even larger total likelihood.)*

## 4. Log-Likelihood & Optimization ($\arg\max$)

### Why Use Log-Likelihood?

Multiplying many small probabilities together is computationally inconvenient and can cause numerical underflow (numbers rounding to $0$). Taking the natural logarithm turns products into sums:

$$\ell(\beta) = \log L(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ Y_i \log(p_i) + (1 - Y_i) \log(1 - p_i) \Big]$$

*(Notice how the switch still works: if $Y_i = 1$, then $(1 - Y_i) = 0$, zeroing out the second term and leaving only $\log(p_i)$.)*

### Finding the Optimal Weights ($\arg\max$)

Because the logarithm is a strictly increasing function, maximizing $\ell(\beta)$ produces the exact same optimal weights ($\hat{\beta}$) as maximizing $L(\beta)$:

$$\hat{\beta} = \arg\max_{\beta} \ell(\beta)$$

* $\arg\max_{\beta}$ ("argument of the maximum") means: *return the specific coefficient values $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_n$ that produce the highest possible log-likelihood score.*
