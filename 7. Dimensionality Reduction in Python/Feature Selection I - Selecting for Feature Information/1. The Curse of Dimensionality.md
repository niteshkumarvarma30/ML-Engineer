# Concept

## The Curse of Dimensionality

As a Data Scientist, your first instinct is usually: "More data is always better! Give me more columns!" While more rows (observations) are always good, adding more columns (features) can actually destroy your model. This trap is known as **The Curse of Dimensionality**.

Here is a breakdown of why it happens and how to spot it.

---

### 1. Memorizing vs. Learning Patterns

Machine learning models are basically lazy students. If you don't give them enough data, they will just memorize the answers instead of learning the actual formulas.

**Scenario A: Too Few Observations**
Look at the first table in your screenshots. You have exactly two houses:
*   Berlin = 2 Million Euros
*   Paris = 3 Million Euros

If you train a model on this, the plot is just two flat, dull blocks. The model hasn't learned anything about real estate. It just memorized: *"If Price = 2, guess Berlin. If Price = 3, guess Paris."* When a new house in Berlin pops up costing 2.5 Million, the model will be completely confused and fail.

![Scenario A: Too Few Observations](images/Screenshot%202026-07-09%20111928.png)

**Scenario B: Finding the Pattern**
Look at the second table. We expanded to 1,000 observations per city.
Now, the plot shows two beautiful, overlapping bell curves (Distributions). The model can finally see that Berlin houses generally hover around 2-4 Million, and Paris houses hover around 3-6 Million. It has stopped memorizing and started generalizing.

![Scenario B: Finding the Pattern](images/Screenshot%202026-07-09%20111941.png)

---

### 2. Building a Classifier (The Sanity Check)

To prove our model is actually generalizing and not just memorizing, we use the `train_test_split` trick. We train it on 70% of the data, and test it on 30% of the data it has never seen before.

Here is the code to build a Support Vector Machine (SVC) city classifier:

```python
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score

# 1. Split Features (X) and Target (y)
y = house_df['City']
X = house_df.drop('City', axis=1)

# 2. Chop the data into a 70% Train / 30% Test split
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3)

# 3. Fit the model to the training data
svc = SVC()
svc.fit(X_train, y_train)

# 4. The Sanity Check (Evaluating Accuracy)
print("Test Score:", accuracy_score(y_test, svc.predict(X_test)))   # Output: 0.826
print("Train Score:", accuracy_score(y_train, svc.predict(X_train))) # Output: 0.832
```

**How to read these scores:**
*   The model scored **83.2%** on the practice test (Training Data).
*   The model scored **82.6%** on the final exam (Test Data).

**The Verdict:** Because the scores are incredibly close to each other, the model is perfectly healthy! It successfully learned the general pattern. If it had memorized the data, the Training score would be 99%, and the Test score would be 50% (Overfitting).

---

### 3. The Curse of Dimensionality (The Trap)

An 82.6% accuracy is good, but we want to make it better.

Your instinct is to add more clues (features). As shown in your final screenshot, you add `n_floors`, `n_bathrooms`, and `surface_m2`.

**The Trap:**
Every time you add a new feature to your dataset, you are creating exponentially more unique combinations.
*   A "2 Million Euro" house is common.
*   A "2 Million Euro house, with 3 floors, 4 bathrooms, 240 surface m2, a red door, and a gravel driveway" is completely, 100% mathematically unique.

If you keep adding features, every single house in your dataset becomes a completely unique fingerprint. The model will revert to being a lazy student. It will stop looking for general patterns and simply memorize the unique fingerprints of your training data. It will overfit wildly.

> **The Golden Rule:** To avoid the Curse of Dimensionality, the number of rows (observations) in your dataset must increase exponentially for every new column (feature) you add. If you don't have millions of rows of data to support your 100 columns, you must use Dimensionality Reduction (like Feature Selection or PCA) to delete those extra columns!
