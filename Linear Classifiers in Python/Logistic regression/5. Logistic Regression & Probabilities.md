# Concept

## Logistic Regression & Probabilities

So far, we know that Logistic Regression draws a straight line in the sand (the Decision Boundary).
* If you are on the right side, it makes a "hard" prediction of Class 1.
* If you are on the left side, it makes a "hard" prediction of Class 0.

But how does it calculate the percentages when we use the `predict_proba()` function?

---

### 1. The Sigmoid "Squisher" Function

In our math lesson, we learned that a linear classifier calculates a **Raw Model Output** by multiplying weights and features together `((Coefficients @ Features) + Intercept)`.

The problem is that this Raw Output can be literally any number. It could be +50, -1,200, or +3.14.
You can't tell a human, *"I am +50 confident this patient has a tumor."* That makes no sense. We need a percentage between 0% and 100% (or 0.0 to 1.0).

**The Solution:** The model takes that Raw Output number and feeds it into the **Sigmoid Function**.
Think of the Sigmoid Function as a magical mathematical "Squisher" (it looks exactly like the blue S-Curve from your earlier images!).

* **If the Raw Output is exactly 0:** It squishes it to `0.50` (50%). You are standing exactly on the boundary line!
* **If the Raw Output is a large Positive number:** It squishes it closer and closer to `1.0` (100%). You are deep in Class 1 territory.
* **If the Raw Output is a large Negative number:** It squishes it closer and closer to `0.0` (0%). You are deep in Class 0 territory.

The further away you walk from the 50% boundary line, the more confident the model becomes!

---

### 2. Regularization prevents "Arrogance"

In the last chapter, we learned that Regularization (controlled by the `C` hyperparameter) shrinks the model's coefficients to prevent overfitting. Now we finally see *why* this prevents overfitting!

Imagine a model with massive, un-regularized coefficients.
Because the coefficients are so huge, the model takes one tiny step away from the boundary line and the Raw Output instantly shoots up to +10,000. The Sigmoid function squishes that to `0.999999`.

**The Danger:** The model is incredibly overconfident (arrogant). It jumps to 100% certainty immediately. Overconfidence is a massive warning sign of overfitting.

**How Regularization Fixes This:**
When we apply regularization, we shrink the coefficients. Now, when the model takes a step away from the boundary line, the Raw Output only grows to +2. The Sigmoid function squishes that to `0.75` (75%).

**The Result:** The model is more cautious and less arrogant. Its probabilities hover closer to the 50% mark instead of jumping to extremes. This cautiousness makes it much smarter when facing real-world, unseen data!

---

### 3. The Two Jobs of Coefficients

You might be wondering: If a model only needs one number to draw the slope of a straight line, why does a 2-feature model have 2 separate coefficients?

This transcript finally answers that! The two coefficients work together as a team to do two completely different jobs:

* **The Ratio (The Angle):** The ratio between the two coefficients (e.g., one is twice as big as the other) determines the **Angle/Slope** of the decision boundary line on the graph.
* **The Magnitude (The Confidence):** The actual size of the coefficients (e.g., 200 and 100 vs. 2 and 1) determines the **Confidence**. Larger numbers mean the model gets extremely confident very quickly as you move away from the line.

By tweaking the regularization (`C`), we can control the *magnitude* of those coefficients, effectively controlling how "confident" our model is allowed to be!
