# TF-IDF (Text Vectorization)

In our previous lesson, we translated text into math using a simple Word Count (`CountVectorizer`). We just tallied up how many times every word was used.

But simple counting has a massive flaw.

## 1. The Problem: The "The" Effect

Look at your first screenshot (Tf-Idf Representation).

If you count the raw words in a President's speech, the word "the" appears 9,795 times. The word "and" appears 5,000 times.
Because machine learning models think Bigger Number = More Important, the model will look at this and say: "Wow! This speech is entirely about the concept of 'the' and 'and'!"

This is completely useless. We need a way to mathematically crush these common, boring words, while boosting the rare, unique words that actually tell us what the speech is about.

## 2. The Solution: The TF-IDF Formula

We solve this using TF-IDF (Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency).

Here is how the formula works in plain English:

### Part 1: TF (Term Frequency)

*   **The Question:** How many times does the word appear in this specific document?
*   **The Math:** (Count of word in the document) / (Total words in the document)

If the word "Economy" is used 20 times in a short speech, its TF score goes up.

### Part 2: IDF (Inverse Document Frequency)

*   **The Question:** How rare is this word across every single document in our entire dataset?
*   **The Math:** `log(Total number of documents / Number of documents containing the word)`

If the word "the" appears in 100% of all speeches, its IDF score gets crushed to almost 0.

### The Final Score (TF x IDF)

The algorithm multiplies the two scores together.
If a word is used a lot in one specific speech (High TF) **AND** it is very rarely used in other speeches (High IDF), it gets a massive mathematical score. The algorithm realizes: *"This word is highly unique and incredibly important to this specific speaker!"*

## 3. How to use it in Scikit-Learn

Look at your Importing the vectorizer and Max features and stopwords screenshots. It works almost exactly like the `CountVectorizer`!

**The Two Pro Hyperparameters:**

*   `max_features=100`: To prevent our spreadsheet from getting millions of columns, we tell the computer to only keep the top 100 highest-scoring words across the dataset.
*   `stop_words='english'`: This tells the vectorizer to automatically delete the most common, useless English words (like "the", "a", "and", "is") before it even starts doing the math!

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
import pandas as pd

# 1. Instantiate the Vectorizer
tv = TfidfVectorizer(max_features=100, stop_words='english')

# 2. Fit and Transform the Training Data
train_tv = tv.fit_transform(train_speech_df['text_clean'])

# 3. Glue the math back into a Pandas DataFrame
# (We use .toarray() to unpack the sparse matrix, and .get_feature_names() for the column headers)
train_tv_df = pd.DataFrame(train_tv.toarray(), columns=tv.get_feature_names()).add_prefix('TFIDF_')
```

```python
# Look at the first row (iloc[0]), sort the highest scores to the top, and print the top 5!
```

Success! The boring words like "the" are completely gone. The computer successfully used TF-IDF to realize that George Washington's speech was heavily focused on government, public duty, and the country!

## 5. The Golden Rule for New Data

Just like Scalers and Imputers, the `TfidfVectorizer` learns its math (the IDF vocabulary averages) from the Training Data.

If you want to evaluate new, unseen Test Data, you MUST NOT use `.fit()` or `.fit_transform()`. You must only use `.transform()` on the test set so the model uses the exact same vocabulary rules it memorized from the training set!
