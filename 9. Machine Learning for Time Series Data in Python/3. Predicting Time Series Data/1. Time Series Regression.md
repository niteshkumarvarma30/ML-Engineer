# Time Series Regression

Up until now, we have focused on Classification (predicting a specific category, like whether a heartbeat is "Normal" or "Abnormal").

But what if you want to predict the exact price of a stock tomorrow, or the exact temperature next week? For continuous numbers, we switch to Regression.

## 1. Classification vs. Regression

*(Look at your first screenshot: Classification vs. Regression)*

*   **Classification Output:** `array([0, 1, 1, 0])` (Hard categories. E.g., 0 = No, 1 = Yes).
*   **Regression Output:** `array([0.2, 1.4, 3.6, 0.6])` (Continuous, fine-grained numbers).

Because time series data naturally fluctuates up and down on a continuous scale, Regression is often the perfect tool to model it!

## 2. Correlation vs. Regression

People often confuse these two terms.

*   **Correlation** is just a simple statistic. It tells you if two things move together (e.g., "When X goes up, Y goes up").
*   **Regression** is a formal machine learning model. It learns the exact mathematical rules of the relationship so it can actively predict future values.

### Visualizing Moving Relationships

With time series, relationships change over time. Two stocks might be completely uncorrelated in 2010, but move in perfect sync by 2015.

*(Look at your Visualizing relationships between timeseries screenshots)*

There are two great ways to plot this:

*   **Double Line Plot:** Plot both variables as lines on the same X-axis (time) to see if their peaks and valleys match up.
*   **Color-Encoded Scatterplot:** Plot Variable X against Variable Y, but use Color to represent the passage of time (e.g., dark purple is the past, bright yellow is the present). If the bright yellow dots suddenly form a straight diagonal line, it proves the two variables recently became highly correlated!

### B. Ridge Regression (Handling Noise)

*(See your Visualize predictions screenshots)*

Sometimes, raw time series data is incredibly noisy. If we use standard `LinearRegression`, the predictions will jump around wildly.

To fix this, we use Ridge Regression. It acts like a shock-absorber. It has an `alpha` parameter that mathematically smooths out the coefficients. By testing different alpha values (like 0.1, 100, and 1000), you can force the model to output a much smoother, safer prediction line over time!

## 4. Grading the Model: R-Squared ($R^2$)

In Classification, we graded our models using "Accuracy" (what percentage did you get right?).
In Regression, you can't use Accuracy. (If a stock costs \$150.00 and you predicted \$150.01, you are technically "wrong", but that is still a great prediction!).

Instead, the most common metric we use is the Coefficient of Determination, better known as $R^2$ (R-Squared).

*(Look at the formula in your final screenshots)*

$1 - \frac{\text{error(model)}}{\text{variance(testdata)}}$

### How to read the R-Squared Score:

It basically asks: *"How much better is my machine learning model compared to a 'dumb' model that just guesses the average every single time?"*

*   **Score = 1.0:** Perfect! Your model predicted every single fluctuation flawlessly.
*   **Score between 0.0 and 1.0:** The closer to 1.0, the better the model is at predicting the outputs.
*   **Score = 0.0:** Your model is totally useless. It is no better than just guessing the average.
*   **Score < 0.0 (Negative):** Your model is actually worse than just guessing the average. (Yes, models can be infinitely bad!).

### How to code it:

```python
from sklearn.metrics import r2_score

# Pass the model's predictions first, and the true answers second!
score = r2_score(y_predicted, y_test)
print(score)
```
