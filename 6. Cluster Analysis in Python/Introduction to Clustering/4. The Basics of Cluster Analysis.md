# Concept

## The Basics of Cluster Analysis

We know that Clustering is the process of grouping unlabeled data points together based on their similarities (like grouping news articles by topic, or customers by spending habits).

But how does the computer actually figure out how to group them?
In this course, we focus on the two most famous clustering algorithms: **Hierarchical** and **K-Means**. They achieve the same goal, but they use completely different logic!

---

### 1. Hierarchical Clustering (The "Bottom-Up" Method)

Hierarchical clustering builds clusters step-by-step by slowly merging data points together.

#### How the Algorithm Thinks:
Imagine you have 13 people standing in a field, and you want to group them into 3 teams.

* **Every person is their own team:** The algorithm starts by pretending there are 13 separate clusters.
* **Find the closest pair:** It measures the distance between everyone. It finds the two people standing closest to each other and tells them to hold hands. They are now a single cluster of 2!
* **Find the new center:** It calculates the exact middle point between those two people. That middle point is the new "Center" of that cluster.
* **Repeat:** It looks for the next closest points (or clusters) and merges them. Every time a merge happens, the total number of clusters drops by one (13 $\rightarrow$ 12 $\rightarrow$ 11...).
* **Stop:** It keeps merging until it hits the target number you asked for (in this case, 3 clusters).

#### SciPy Code Cheat Sheet:
To do this in Python, we use the SciPy library (specifically `scipy.cluster.hierarchy`).
* `linkage()`: This is the math engine. It computes all the distances and figures out who should merge with who.
* `fcluster()`: This generates the final labels (e.g., assigning every point a 1, 2, or 3) so you can plot them on a graph!

---

### 2. K-Means Clustering (The "Random Pins" Method)

K-Means is incredibly fast and approaches the problem from the top down. The "K" just stands for the number of clusters you want to find.

#### How the Algorithm Thinks:
Imagine the same 13 people in a field, and you want to find 3 teams ($K=3$).

* **The Random Guess:** The computer closes its eyes and throws 3 random pins onto the field. These pins are the starting "Cluster Centers" (Centroids).
* **The Assignment:** Every person looks at the 3 pins and walks over to whichever pin is physically closest to them. We now have 3 rough groups.
* **The Adjustment:** The pins look at the people standing around them. Each pin moves itself to stand perfectly in the dead-center (the average) of its group.
* **Repeat:** Because the pins moved, some people might realize a different pin is now closer to them! They switch groups. The pins move again to find the new center.
* **Stop:** This repeats until nobody switches groups and the pins stop moving. The clusters are locked in!

#### SciPy Code Cheat Sheet:
To do this in Python, we use `scipy.cluster.vq`.
* `kmeans()`: This calculates where the final pins (the Centroids) should go. (Note: It also spits out a "distortion" score, which tells you how tightly packed the clusters are).
* `vq()`: This stands for Vector Quantization. It actually assigns each data point to the closest pin, giving you your final labels!

---

### Visualizing the Results

For both of these algorithms, the output is just a list of numbers (labels). To actually see if the clusters make sense, we use libraries like `matplotlib` or `seaborn` (e.g., `sns.scatterplot()`).

By setting the `hue` argument to the cluster labels, the graph will automatically color-code the dots so your human eyes can instantly verify if the computer's math was correct!
