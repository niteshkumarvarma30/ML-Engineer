# Concept

## Intro to Unsupervised Learning

In our previous courses (like XGBoost, Random Forests, and Logistic Regression), we always had an "answer key." We told the computer: *"Here is a house's square footage, and here is its exact price. Learn the pattern."*

But what happens when you have a massive pile of data and **no answer key**? You use **Unsupervised Learning**!

---

### 1. Labeled vs. Unlabeled Data

To understand Unsupervised Learning, you must understand the data it uses.

* **Labeled Data:** The data has a predefined answer or category attached to it.
  * *Example:* A list of coordinates on a map, where every coordinate is already explicitly tagged as "Safe Zone" or "Danger Zone" by a human.

* **Unlabeled Data:** The data is just raw information with *no answers attached*.
  * *Example:* Just a giant list of X and Y coordinates on a map. No tags, no colors, no context.

**Unsupervised Learning algorithms strictly use Unlabeled Data.** You hand the computer a giant, messy spreadsheet and say: *"I don't know what I'm looking at. Please organize this and tell me if you find any hidden patterns."*

---

### 2. Real-World Example: Google News

If you go to Google News, you will see a headline like "Indian Cricket Team Wins Match," and right below it, you will see 5 other articles from different websites about the exact same topic.

Did a human at Google manually read those 5 articles and label them "Cricket"? No! 
Google uses an unsupervised learning algorithm. The computer scans millions of articles, counts the words, and automatically groups them together based on similar word patterns. It doesn't know what cricket is, it just knows those articles belong together.

---

### 3. What is Clustering?

The Google News example relies on the most famous type of Unsupervised Learning: **Clustering**.

Clustering is the act of grouping items together so that:
* Items in the **same group** are incredibly similar to each other.
* Items in **different groups** are incredibly different from each other.

---

### 4. Visualizing Clusters (The Pokemon Example)

Look at the image you uploaded with the blue dots and red circles.

Imagine you are playing Pokémon GO in a park. You walk around and log the exact X and Y GPS coordinates every time a Pokémon appears. 
You now have a spreadsheet of 15 coordinates. This is unlabeled data (you don't know *what* Pokémon they are, just *where* they appeared).

If you plot those coordinates on a scatter plot, human eyes can instantly see a pattern. The Pokémon aren't spawning randomly everywhere; they are spawning in **3 distinct clusters** (the red circles).

Unsupervised learning algorithms are designed to do exactly this: they look at raw numbers and mathematically draw those "red circles" to discover the natural groupings in the world!
